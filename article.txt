Introduction
============
The COVID-19 pandemic has generated an unprecedented volume of clinical data within healthcare systems worldwide, making retrospective analysis essential for understanding disease dynamics. Hospital information systems have accumulated extensive patient records, ranging from basic demographics to complex longitudinal laboratory measurements. However, extracting reliable and clinically meaningful insights from real-world medical records remains a significant challenge due to high levels of noise, irregular sampling, and a substantial proportion of missing values.

In clinical practice, time-to-event (TTE) data are particularly relevant, as they describe the interval between a starting point (e.g., admission) and an event of interest, such as mortality. Unlike standard classification tasks, survival analysis must appropriately handle right-censored observations - cases where the event was not observed during the study period, such as patients discharged alive.

This study analyzes clinical data from COVID-19 patients hospitalized at the Louis Pasteur University Hospital (UNLP) in Košice. Our primary motivation is a deep retrospective comparison between the first and fourth pandemic waves to identify how patient profiles and survival probabilities evolved over time. Based on consultations with medical experts, we focus on identifying specific biomarkers that most significantly influenced the clinical course of the disease.

The main objective is to evaluate advanced survival models capable of handling high-dimensional and incomplete medical records. We propose an integrated pipeline that utilizes the Gated Recurrent Unit with Decay (GRU-D) architecture for temporal imputation of missing values. This is combined with Extreme Gradient Boosting models with two different strategies (XGB_COX and XGB_AF) and Random Survival Forests (RSF). To ensure clinical relevance, we employ SHAP-based analysis to quantify the contribution of individual biomarkers to predicted survival times, providing actionable insights for risk stratification.

Materials and Methods
=====================
Data Source and Cohort Selection
--------------------------------
The clinical data for this study were provided by the Louis Pasteur University Hospital (UNLP) in Košice, originally spanning four distinct COVID-19 pandemic waves. After consultation with hospital clinicians, the research focused on a comparative analysis of the first and fourth waves to observe the evolution of the disease and patient responses from the start to the later stages of the pandemic. The first wave cohort consisted of 1,124 patients, while the fourth wave included 1,251 patients. The target attribute for survival analysis was in-hospital mortality, with recorded deaths as follows: 132 in the first wave and 131 in the fourth wave. For modeling purposes, discharge or transfer to another department was treated as a right-censored observation.

Demographic and Clinical Profile
--------------------------------
A comparative analysis of the patient cohorts reveals a profound shift in age distribution between the two pandemic waves. Although both waves included patients across the entire spectrum from 19 to 99 years, the concentration within specific age groups evolved significantly.
The first wave cohort was characterized by a broader distribution across middle and older age categories, with the average age being 62 years. The number of hospitalized patients increased steadily across the younger and middle-age brackets: 18 patients in the [19, 29) group, 62 in the [29, 39) group, 143 in the [39, 49) group, and 202 in the [49, 59) range. The population peaked in the [59, 69) age group with 277 patients, followed by 248 patients in the [69, 79) range. The oldest categories were less represented, with 141 patients in the [79, 89) group and 32 patients in the [89, 99) group.
In contrast, the fourth wave showed a marked decline in the hospitalization of younger and middle-aged individuals, while the elderly population became dominant, shifting the average age to 72 years,. The counts for younger groups were significantly lower than in the first wave: 12 patients in the [19, 29) group, 33 in the [29, 39) group, 70 in the [39, 49) group, and 91 in the [49, 59) group. A moderate increase was seen in the [59, 69) group with 189 patients. However, the most critical shift occurred in the oldest brackets, where the [69, 79) group rose to 370 patients, the [79, 89) group surged to 393 patients, and the [89, 99) group nearly tripled to 91 patients.

Data Preprocessing Pipeline
---------------------------
The data preparation process was conducted in four rigorous stages, each validated by medical experts to ensure the robustness and clinical relevance of the resulting survival models. Initially, model stability was preserved by excluding eight laboratory markers—specifically S-Bil-D S-KM, S-LD, S-AMS, S-IgG, S-IgM, S-Chol and S-IgA due to excessive missingness ranging from 21.9% to 97.5%.

The second stage involved the removal of invalid records where numerical results were replaced by textual descriptions of collection failures. A comparative analysis of these errors reveals that the incidence was significantly higher during the first wave compared to the fourth, even though the fourth wave had a larger total patient count. The database contained over thirty unique types of textual error labels, ranging from specific laboratory failures to qualitative descriptions of results.
The most dominant reason for invalidity was a "coagulated sample", accounting for nearly 700 entries in the first wave versus 550 in the fourth. Similarly, "hemolysis" was twice as frequent in the first wave (approximately 400 cases) as in the fourth (200 cases). While most error categories were more prevalent in the early stages, certain unique labels such as "will be processed" or specific threshold indicators like ">35000" showed a higher frequency during the fourth wave.
Regarding individual clinical parameters, the most significant burden of invalid data was concentrated in coagulation and inflammatory markers. Tests such as PT (INR), APTT-R, D-dimer HS, Fibrinogen, and Plasma Lactate were the most problematic, with invalid measurement counts per test ranging between 100 and 220 in the first wave. In contrast, the maximum number of invalid readings for any single test in the fourth wave did not exceed 150. For the majority of other laboratory parameters, the frequency of errors was significantly lower, typically oscillating around 50 or fewer entries per test. These disparities likely reflect the extreme administrative and laboratory pressures during the early pandemic, while the overall decline in errors during the later phase suggests a gradual improvement in hospital processes and staff proficiency.

Temporal Synchronization and Hospitalization Filtering
------------------------------------------------------
To address temporal inconsistencies and ensure data robustness for longitudinal modeling, several adjustments were made to the laboratory records' timestamps. In standard clinical practice at the monitored department, laboratory tests were typically administered once per day for the majority of patients. However, the original timestamps in the hospital information system frequently reflected administrative entry times rather than the exact moment of clinical sample collection.
A significant challenge was the irregularity of the testing schedule, as various biomarkers were not always measured simultaneously. Retaining precise hours and minutes would have resulted in a highly sparse dataset with numerous "gaps" or empty records for each patient trajectory. Although a subset of patients in critical condition—representing approximately 2.5% of the measurements—underwent testing two to three times within a single 24-hour period, this small fraction did not justify the use of high-frequency temporal modeling. Such precision would have necessitated an excessive volume of data imputation, potentially introducing artificial bias and decreasing the reliability of the models.
Consequently, to minimize the missingness and streamline the trajectories, all measurements were aggregated to a daily level, and specific time components were disregarded. During this process, for patients with multiple daily measurements, we purposefully retained only the last recorded value to represent the most recent physiological state before the next observation window. This strategic synchronization effectively reduced the number of rows per patient, simplifying the imputation process and thereby increasing the overall consistency and reliability of the longitudinal analysis.
This temporal refinement was strictly coupled with a hospitalization filter to ensure that only relevant clinical data were analyzed. In consultation with medical experts, all measurements recorded outside the interval between a patient’s admission and discharge were excluded. The impact of this filter was particularly significant in the first wave, where the top fourteen laboratory tests each had between 1,000 and 1,700 records removed, whereas in the fourth wave, no single test exceeded 600 removed records. Due to the total absence of valid clinical data within the stay interval after filtering, 4 patients from the first wave and 7 from the fourth wave were excluded from further study.
The final evaluation of the data volume illustrates a shift in monitoring practices. While the first wave initially contained more raw measurements, the application of the hospitalization filter reversed the trend. The final dataset for the fourth wave retained 116,762 measurements, surpassing the 104,935 measurements of the first wave, suggesting more intensive and better-targeted clinical monitoring during the later pandemic phase.

Experimental Pipeline nd Model Construction
===========================================
To ensure the reproducibility and consistency of our results, we developed a custom technical solution (an automated pipeline) designed to manage the entire experimental workflow, from raw data processing to final model evaluation. This architecture was specifically built to handle the complexities of longitudinal medical records, where a single patient contributes multiple daily observations rather than a single static entry.

Data Integrity and Validation Strategy
--------------------------------------
To prevent data leakage, it was critical to ensure that records belonging to the same individual were not split between the training and testing sets. Each patient was assigned a unique identifier, allowing us to utilize the StratifiedGroupKFold strategy from the scikit-learn library. This approach guarantees that all daily trajectories for any given patient remain within a single fold while simultaneously maintaining the same proportion of the target event (mortality) across all subsets.

The Integrated Modeling Architecture
------------------------------------
The pipeline operates in several synchronized stages, utilizing configuration files (YAML) to define the search space for hyperparameter optimization:
Temporal Imputation (GRU-D): The first stage employs the Gated Recurrent Unit with Decay (GRU-D) architecture. Unlike static imputation methods, GRU-D explicitly models the decay of information over time, which is essential for irregular laboratory measurements. The hyperparameters for the imputer were optimized using the Optuna framework to minimize validation loss.
Survival Modeling: Once the trajectories are imputed, the complete data are passed to the survival models. For this study, we evaluated three distinct modeling approaches:
XGB_AFT: Extreme Gradient Boosting with an Accelerated Failure Time objective, which directly models survival time.
Random Survival Forest (RSF): A non-parametric ensemble method that averages cumulative hazard functions across multiple trees.
XGB_COX: A gradient boosting model based on the Cox Proportional Hazards regression. This model was included as a comparative baseline due to the widespread use of Cox regression in existing clinical survival studies.

Hyperparameter Optimization and Robustness
------------------------------------------
The pipeline utilizes Optuna for the simultaneous or sequential tuning of both the GRU-D imputer and the final survival models. To ensure the statistical reliability of our findings, we executed multiple experimental runs for each pandemic wave and each model. In total, the pipeline processed 120 experiments (10 runs per wave for each of the three models), allowing us to assess the stability and robustness of the predictive performance across different temporal cohorts.

----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Results
=======
Comparative Performance and Baseline Evaluation
-----------------------------------------------
To assess the predictive capability of the proposed pipeline, we evaluated three distinct survival modeling approaches across 120 experimental runs. The XGB_COX model was included as a comparative baseline due to the widespread use of Cox regression in clinical studies. However, its performance was significantly limited; in the first wave, it achieved a mean test C-index of only 0.551 (± 0.007), while in the fourth wave, it reached 0.650 (± 0.005). Due to this consistently low discriminative power, XGB_COX was excluded from further interpretability analysis.
In contrast, XGB_AFT and RSF demonstrated high and stable performance. For the first wave, XGB_AFT achieved a mean test C-index of 0.864 (± 0.18), slightly outperforming RSF at 0.845 (± 0.16). A general decline was observed in the fourth wave, where XGB_AFT dropped to 0.770 (± 0.68) and RSF to 0.758 (± 0.13). This trend reflects the increased complexity and heterogeneity of patient profiles in the later stages of the pandemic.

Model Calibration and Selection Strategy
----------------------------------------
Model reliability was evaluated using the Integrated Brier Score (IBS) for RSF, yielding 0.091 for the first wave and 0.120 for the fourth. These values are significantly more accurate than the random guess threshold of 0.25. For clinical interpretability, we selected models with the highest generalization capability (minimal training-test gap). For XGB_AFT, this was validated by AIC; in the first wave, we selected a model with an AIC of 1310.65 (range: 1299.52–1317.60), and in the fourth, a model with an AIC of 1748.04 (range: 1744.08–1753.37).

Risk Stratification and Clinical
--------------------------------
Progression The discriminative power of the selected XGB_AFT models is shown in Figure 1. The models achieved a clear separation (p<0.001) between high-risk and low-risk groups. In the first wave, mortality was concentrated in the high-risk group (114 deaths) compared to the low-risk group (4 deaths). In the fourth wave, even the low-risk group showed a survival decline similar to the high-risk group of the first wave, suggesting a more aggressive disease course. Figure 2 demonstrates that the RSF model accurately tracked the actual clinical progression (Kaplan-Meier ground truth). Prediction error over time, quantified by the Brier Score in Figure 3, remained low, peaking at 0.11 in the first wave and 0.21 in the fourth.

SHAP-Based Interpretability and Thresholds
------------------------------------------
SHAP analysis (Figures 4 and 5) identified critical laboratory thresholds. A key finding is that several "critical breakpoints" occurred within standard physiological ranges. In the first wave, S-Urea (7.39 mmol/l) and S-AST (0.81 μkat/l) signaled increased risk while still near the upper limit of the norm. In the fourth wave, this was more pronounced for S-Na (141.34 mmol/l) and S-CK-MB (0.29 μkat/l). Immune depletion markers, such as CD3+ (threshold 0.65) in the first wave and CD4+ (0.54) or Eo abs (0.02) in the fourth, showed that any decline below the norm was a high-risk signal.

Discussion
==========
7.1. Model Performance and Predictive Complexity The results of this study demonstrate that advanced machine learning ensembles, specifically XGB_AFT and RSF, significantly outperform the traditional XGB_COX baseline in predicting COVID-19 survival. The consistently low C-index of the XGB_COX model (~0.55 in Wave 1) suggests that the proportional hazards assumption may not adequately capture the non-linear dynamics of longitudinal clinical data. In contrast, the high discriminative power achieved by XGB_AFT (C-index 86.45% in Wave 1) confirms the effectiveness of modeling survival time directly.
A critical observation is the performance decline and increased complexity noted in the fourth pandemic wave. The decrease in C-index (from 86.4% to 77.0%) and the corresponding rise in AIC values (from ~1310 to ~1748) indicate that survival prediction became inherently more challenging as the pandemic evolved. This shift likely reflects greater patient heterogeneity, including varying vaccination statuses and the emergence of more aggressive viral variants, which introduced higher uncertainty into the clinical records.
7.2. Prediction Reliability and Risk Stratification The reliability of our models is further supported by the Integrated Brier Score (IBS). With values of 0.091 (Wave 1) and 0.120 (Wave 4), the RSF model proved to be more than twice as accurate as a random guess (0.25). The time-dependent Brier Score (Figure 3 / Obr. 17) shows that while predictive error remains low in the early stages of hospitalization, it increases during longer stays, particularly in the fourth wave where it peaked at 0.21.
The clinical utility of these models is best illustrated by the risk stratification in Figure 1 (Obr. 15). The statistically significant separation (p<0.001) between risk groups reveals a "prognostic shift" between waves. Notably, the low-risk group in the fourth wave exhibited a survival trajectory nearly identical to the high-risk group in the first wave. This finding suggests that even patients initially classified as having lower risk in the later stages of the pandemic faced a clinical threat comparable to the most critical cases from the pandemic’s onset.
7.3. Clinical Significance of Laboratory Thresholds The SHAP-based interpretability analysis (Figures 4-5 / Obr. 18-19) provides actionable insights by identifying critical laboratory breakpoints. A primary contribution of this work is the identification of "high-normal" risk signals. The model flagged increased mortality risk for biomarkers such as S-Urea, S-Na, and S-CK-MB even while their values remained within standard physiological reference ranges. For instance, in the fourth wave, sodium (S-Na) levels above 141.34 mmol/l—well within the normal range—already signaled a rapid increase in risk. This suggests that standard hospital alert thresholds may not be sensitive enough for early risk stratification in COVID-19 patients.
Furthermore, the models accurately captured signs of immune exhaustion. For markers like CD3+, CD4+, and absolute eosinophils (Eo abs), the SHAP analysis revealed that any decline below the lower limit of the norm served as a critical indicator of poor prognosis.
7.4. Limitations and Methodological Artifacts Despite the robustness of the pipeline, the S-ALT paradox (where higher values appeared protective) highlights a limitation of working with real-world longitudinal data. Following clinical consultation, this was identified as a potential artifact of the GRU-D temporal imputation in the presence of high missingness for this specific marker. This emphasizes that while machine learning can uncover hidden patterns, expert clinical validation remains essential. Additionally, as the data originates from a single medical center (UNLP), future work should focus on multi-center validation to confirm whether these identified SHAP thresholds are universal across different populations.
